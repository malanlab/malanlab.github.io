## 🧠 Which cortical oscillatory features and brain regions are associated with chronic pain severity?

<div class="card">

### Problem

Chronic pain is associated with abnormal cortical rhythms, particularly in the alpha band (8–12 Hz). However, it remains unclear how peak alpha frequency (PAF) and alpha power change across brain regions, and how these changes relate to subjective pain severity.

### What we studied

We used resting-state MEG in CRPS patients and healthy controls to estimate:

- Peak alpha frequency (PAF)
- Alpha-band power across cortex
- Functional connectivity in alpha networks
- Relationship with pain severity

### Key findings

- CRPS shows **global slowing of peak alpha frequency (PAF)**
- **Reduced alpha power**, especially in:
  - Precuneus  
  - Paracentral cortex  
  - Superior parietal cortex  

- Pain severity is linked to:
  - Reduced PAF power in **prefrontal cortex (PFC)** and **orbitofrontal cortex (OFC)**
  - Increased alpha-band connectivity in PFC–OFC networks

### Interpretation

Alpha oscillations reflect both sensory and affective dimensions of chronic pain:

- Posteromedial regions → sensory-discriminative processing  
- Prefrontal / orbitofrontal cortex → subjective pain intensity  
- Network hyperconnectivity → maladaptive affect integration

</div>

## ⚡ How does the human cerebellum interact with cortex during motor error processing?

<div class="card">

### Problem

The cerebellum is essential for motor learning and error correction, but cerebello–cortical interaction during real-time motor error processing in humans is not fully understood.

### What we study

We investigate cerebello–cortical communication using:

- EEG/MEG recordings
- Motor error paradigms
- Connectivity and oscillatory coupling
- Computational modeling

### Key questions

- How does the cerebellum transmit error signals to cortex?
- Which cortical regions encode motor updates?
- How does learning reshape these interactions?

### Interpretation

We aim to explain how cerebello–cortical loops support:

- Error detection  
- Motor adaptation  
- Trial-by-trial learning  

</div>

## 🚶 How does cortico–subcortical network dysfunction lead to freezing of gait in Parkinson’s disease?

<div class="card">

### Problem

Freezing of gait (FOG) in Parkinson’s disease arises from transient motor block, but its network-level neural mechanism remains unclear.

### What we study

We use:

- EEG and LFP recordings
- Deep brain stimulation (DBS)
- Motor task paradigms
- Beta burst and connectivity analysis

### Key hypotheses

- FOG reflects **disrupted cortico–basal ganglia communication**
- Abnormal **beta bursts** reflect impaired motor state switching
- DBS restores network dynamics and motor flow

### Interpretation

FOG is a **network failure of motor state transitions**, involving cortical, basal ganglia, and DBS-modulated circuits.

</div>
